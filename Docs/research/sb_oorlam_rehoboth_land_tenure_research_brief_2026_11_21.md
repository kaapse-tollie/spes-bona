# Serfdom and pastoral land tenure in Oorlam and Rehoboth

21 November 2026 · Spes Bona research brief for 1836 Oorlam and the later Rehoboth creation event

## Executive summary

**Replace Serfdom for Oorlam (ORL) with a pastoral tenure law.** ORL inherits Serfdom from the traditional starting-politics helper [1, ORL history], [2, starting politics]. The law restricts peasant movement [2, Land Reform and modifier descriptions] and protects subsistence jobs in incorporated states [2, Land Reform and modifier descriptions]. Near-start evidence connects the Afrikaner following to livestock and cattle raids [3, vol. I, pp. 136–137], [4, pp. 568–569]. Studies of the wider polity also identify control of grazing and water at a time of political expansion [5, pp. 93–95], [6, “Oorlam Windhoek”]. The more plausible model is control of access to pastoral resources, with the captain's military power and coerced labour represented through their own systems.

**Give Rehoboth (RHB) communal grazing when the tag forms.** Its history specifies Homesteading after the traditional helper [1, RHB history], and the Cape creation event has no explicit Land Reform assignment [1, Rehoboth Trek]. The historical settlement combined shared pasture [7, pp. 322–323], [8, pp. 141–143] with individual rights to houses or garden plots [7, p. 322], [9, Sec. 7.1.8]. Large-scale grants of private farms began around 1895 [7, pp. 323–325], [8, pp. 142–143]. Homesteading is a useful comparator for that later transition. Verify which law the tag actually receives when the event creates it.

## Introduction

This is a companion to the Basotho Land Reform brief [1, companion brief]. The Oorlam case concerns Jonker Afrikaner's following and its sphere of influence near **1 January 1836**, represented in SB by the decentralised ORL tag in `STATE_HEREROLAND` [1, country definitions, state and character history]. The Rehoboth case interprets “Bastar” as **Rehoboth Basters (RHB)**, SB's decentralised Baster tag [1, country definitions and RHB history]. Its decision point is the foundation of Rehoboth around 1870 [7, pp. 319–320], [8, pp. 141–142] and the corresponding SB event window of **1867–1871** [1, Rehoboth Trek].

The test is whether access to land depended on personal obligations that justify the game's migration, hiring and qualification restrictions. Here **pastoral tenure** means the rules for using pasture, watering places and settlement sites. The technical baseline is SB **0.20.3**, Victoria 3 **1.15.2**, all DLC and the SB-patched Community Mod Framework [1, 1.15.2 compatibility].

## Research approach

The targeted search on 20–21 November 2026 combined publisher and bibliographic discovery through Cambridge Core, JSTOR records and AfricaBib with public-domain travel accounts, the University of Namibia digital library, NamibLII, the Namibian Environmental Information Service library and the TUCSIN history collection. Searches combined *Oorlam*, *Orlam*, *Jonker Afrikaner*, *commando*, *water*, *captivity*, *Rehoboth*, *De Tuin*, *Paternal Laws* and *communal pasture*. I compared accounts close to the period with later archival and scholarly reconstructions and tracked repeated use of the same source. The body uses Lau's published extract within its visible scope [10, extract]. The technical check traces local history, the event that creates RHB, and installed Vanilla law and production method definitions [1, ORL history, RHB history and Rehoboth Trek], [2, starting politics, Land Reform and subsistence production methods]. The findings describe authored settings and historical evidence.

## Findings

### Oorlam · grazing, cattle and political authority

Alexander's party visited an Afrikaner settlement near the Orange River in December 1836 [3, vol. I, pp. 135–137], [11, Sec. 2.2]. He records exchanging tobacco for milk [3, vol. I, p. 137] and reports a missionary challenging the inhabitants over cattle raids [3, vol. I, p. 137]. Ross independently traces Oorlam raiding and the exchange of captured cattle for arms and other goods from the 1830s [4, pp. 568–569]. The two sources support an economy that combined livestock use with access to wealth through commandos and exchange. The visit gives a narrow view of one settlement, and the exchange is evidence about the activity observed there.

For the wider region, Siiskonen's account describes Oorlam control of watering places and pasture and the making of alliances with Nama leaders [5, pp. 93–95]. His explanation of the early decades draws heavily on Lau [5, pp. 93–94, notes 28–33]. Galton's later account likewise reports an alliance with Nama groups and cattle taken in raids traded for guns and horses [12, pp. 69–71]. Galton describes the Swakop's watering places as important on the Nama–Herero frontier [12, pp. 41–42]. Roland, Stevens and Simon's mapping adds the location of water, pasture and routes to the explanation of why Windhoek mattered [6, “Pre-colonial Windhoek” and “Oorlam Windhoek”]. These are overlapping lines of evidence about command of resources and routes. They give little basis for reconstructing precise rules of title or peasant mobility throughout ORL's game territory.

The consolidation of Windhoek should be read separately from the opening date. Alexander's 1836 settlement visit was well to the south [3, vol. I, pp. 135–137], [11, Sec. 2.2]. Siiskonen places Jonker's developed Windhoek residence in the early 1840s [5, p. 93], the period when Dierks traces mission access and the development of roads from Windhoek [11, Sec. 2.3]. Roland and colleagues date Jonker's settlement at the site to 1842 [6, “Oorlam Windhoek”]. Treat the opening capital as a map abstraction for a developing sphere of influence. The established Windhoek economy is later evidence.

### Oorlam · service, captivity and the limits of the Serfdom analogy

The strongest analogy is coercive dependence. Afrikaner influence extended beyond its core through raids and the subordination or displacement of neighbouring communities [13, pp. 428–431], [5, pp. 93–95]. Lau's reconstruction also identifies Herero client chiefs in the Afrikaner coalition by the 1860s [10, extract]. Gewald challenges an older account of the region by tracing the seizure and sale of captives along Cape-linked trade routes [13, pp. 417–419, 429–436]. One of his examples comes from Alexander's account of a raid in which women were assaulted and livestock was taken [3, vol. I, pp. 140–141], [13, pp. 429–430]. The accounts share one witness for that raid. Gewald's wider evidence establishes a regional practice, with less certainty about the scale and organisation of bondage within Jonker's own following.

Galton describes merchants selling on credit and Jonker and others falling into debt [12, pp. 70–72]. The passages on captive-taking and trade describe a different relationship [13, pp. 429–436]. SB's ORL history uses Debt Slavery to represent captivity [1, ORL history], while Vanilla describes the law as forfeiture of the debtor's person to a creditor [2, slavery law descriptions]. The current name is an imperfect proxy. The evidence supports targeted coercion and captivity. It gives weaker support for a general regime in which cultivators owed service on hereditary estates or were barred from changing employment.

### Rehoboth · migration, membership and mixed forms of tenure

The group that established Rehoboth came from De Tuin and began moving north in 1868 [14, historical background], [7, pp. 319–320]. The High Court's historical account describes negotiations with the Swartbooi Nama for land [14, historical background]. Rehoboth began receiving these Baster settlers around 1870 [7, pp. 319–320], [8, pp. 141–142], in a region with existing Nama and Damara settlements [9, Sec. 7.1.8]. The community revised a constitution drafted on the trek and promulgated the Paternal Laws in January 1872 [14, historical background], [7, pp. 319–320]. This establishes a later polity that acquired access collectively and worked out its own institutions as it settled.

Initial land rights combined common pasture [15, p. 223] with household holdings [7, p. 322], [9, Sec. 7.1.8]. Early residents later told the 1930 Land Tenure Inquiry that Baster households had broad choice of where to live [7, p. 322] and treated their houses and gardens as individual property [7, p. 322]. The inquiry testimony describes herds going out together for security [7, p. 322]. The Namibian ancestral land commission identifies probable early individual grants by the Kaptein and council [9, Sec. 7.1.8] and restrictions on transferring house or garden plots outside the citizen body [9, Sec. 7.1.8]. Lang cites an 1872 grazing provision in the Paternal Laws as evidence for access to common pasture at settlement [15, p. 223 and n. 1]. The grant and transfer rules made community membership consequential for access to land [9, Sec. 7.1.8].

### Rehoboth · the move to private ranches

Lang places the beginning of large-scale farm distribution around 1895 using testimony and grants reviewed in the 1930 inquiry [7, pp. 322–325]. Limpricht and Lang identify some earlier grants from the land register [8, pp. 142–143] and reproduce Friedrich Heidmann's 1898 report on the distribution of individually held farms and the risk of their sale or mortgage [8, p. 143]. The two studies share an author. The dated mission correspondence corroborates the transition separately from the later inquiry testimony [8, p. 143], [7, pp. 321–325]. The 2020 commission also records the later issue of title to citizen owners and the exclusion of Nama and Damara occupants from that title distribution [9, Sec. 7.1.8]. The evidence supports a real land reform within the campaign period. Lang connects the timing to military security [7, pp. 322–323], and Heidmann's report shows how the new titles also made sale and mortgage possible in response to debts [8, pp. 143–144]. Use the historical sequence, with room for a different outcome in play.

## Discussion and implications

**ORL needs a law for pastoral access under a captain.** The sources connect power to control of watering places and routes [5, pp. 93–95], [6, “Oorlam Windhoek”] and to obtaining cattle, trade goods and followers through armed organisation [4, pp. 568–569], [12, pp. 69–72], [10, extract]. An estate-style hiring restriction is a poor proxy for those relationships. The uncertainty is how much control over ordinary households a general law should represent. Start with access and mobility. Leave any stronger extraction effects to a later choice with specific evidence.

**RHB needs a law for communal grazing at foundation.** Common use of pasture [7, pp. 322–323], [15, p. 223] and individual house and garden rights [7, p. 322], [9, Sec. 7.1.8] fit together without transferring productive enterprises to their workers. Vanilla's worker ownership allowances are a separate mechanic [2, Land Reform]. Homesteading currently bundles those allowances with a significant arable reservation [2, Land Reform]. Individual farms and the right to dispose of them became a separate historical question after the first settlements [7, pp. 323–325], [8, pp. 142–145].

## Limitations and evidence gaps

- **ORL's early evidence is stronger for the livelihood and power base than for legal tenure.** Alexander was an outside visitor and did not meet the chief at the December 1836 stop [3, vol. I, p. 137]. Galton wrote from a later expedition and retold events before his visit [12, pp. 69–72]. Lau's analysis of the mid-century shift towards raiding and trade is used at the level of the published extract [10, extract]. Siiskonen draws on her earlier research for the 1830s [5, pp. 93–94, notes 28–33]. Estimates of the share of people held in bondage and the rules tying households to particular land remain weak.
- **Rehoboth's evidence is retrospective in part.** The 1930 inquiry interviewed early residents long after founding and operated within a dispute over surveys and access to credit [7, pp. 320–323]. The 2020 commission consulted claims from multiple groups about the same territory [9, Sec. 7.1.8]. The convergence is strongest on mixed founding tenure and a later shift towards private farms. It cannot fix uniform rights at every water point or establish ordinary labour conditions for all residents.
- **The comparison is static.** The event and history establish different authored paths [1, RHB history and Rehoboth Trek]. Effective law on creation, the applicable pasture production method and the employment impact on 1.15.2 remain engine questions. Proposed modifier values are balance hypotheses.

## Recommendations

### 1. Pilot Pastoral Tenure for ORL in 1836

Apply a standalone Land Reform law after the traditional helper in ORL history. Let its description foreground the captain's authority over access to wells and pasture [5, pp. 93–95], [6, “Oorlam Windhoek”], alongside herding and trade [3, vol. I, p. 137], [4, pp. 568–569]. Leave both peasant migration flags and the protected-employment modifier unset. Begin without an extra arable reservation, and test 25 percentage points as a sensitivity check after incorporation if the subsistence economy needs room alongside constructed livestock ranches. Tenant Farmers supplies that numerical comparison [2, Land Reform].

Keep military service, captive labour and trade outside this Land Reform decision. ORL already has a dedicated conscription modifier and a separate slavery law in history [1, ORL history], [2, native conscription]. Review the historical fit of the Debt Slavery label separately if the captivity model changes. Confidence is moderate in the replacement and low in any specific economic bonus.

### 2. Pilot Communal Grazing on RHB's trek route

Represent community control over pasture [7, pp. 322–323], [15, p. 223], with individual holdings for houses and gardens [7, p. 322], [9, Sec. 7.1.8]. Start with the same zero added protected employment and zero added arable reservation as the ORL candidate. Describe who may use the common pasture. Include RHB's existing food-security grant in both sides of the comparison [1, Rehoboth Trek and pastoral modifier]. Leave the candidate's worker-ownership allowance at the default.

Verify what a newly created RHB receives before choosing the implementation point. Give the new law explicitly to a newly formed RHB if the creation path needs it. Test the event with an existing RHB too, so the trek option does not overwrite its subsequent reforms. The history shell and the event should lead to the same founding model wherever each is used. Confidence is high in the historical distinction between common grazing and private ranches, and provisional on the engine path.

### 3. Compare Vanilla and example-mod approaches

| Option | Best use |
| --- | --- |
| **Vanilla Tenant Farmers** | Quick ORL hiring comparator. It has no active protected-employment modifier [2, Land Reform]. It retains an internal peasant migration ban [2, Land Reform] and its description gives hereditary elites ownership [2, law descriptions]. That makes it a weak historical endpoint. |
| **Serfdom variant or Manorialism** | Only consider this for an evidenced regime that warrants estate labour. Manorialism retains the 50-point protected-employment modifier [2, Land Reform] and its Serfdom parent brings it within the farmer qualification check [2, Land Reform and Serfdom trigger]. |
| **Peasant Proprietorship or a local Homesteading-style variant** | Compare these for a later RHB reform towards family ranch ownership, which expanded around 1895 [7, pp. 323–325], [8, pp. 142–143]. Make availability local and decide what boundaries on sale should survive. Verify the desired parent stances, visibility and production method eligibility [2, Land Reform and subsistence production methods], [1, POL-02]. |
| **Custom law or amendment** | Hail Columbia's Land Stewardship demonstrates a titled tenure variant under Collectivised Agriculture [16]. Its worker-ownership modifiers answer a different question [16, law modifiers]. Gates of the Bosphorus uses a polity-specific Çiftlik amendment attached to Serfdom [17]. A similar amendment can represent a later, separately contestable rule for grazing or alienation. Vanilla amendments add modifiers to their host [2, amendment schema], so set the initial migration and employment rules in the base law. |

Reuse an existing customary law family if the intended political stances and legal checks match. Otherwise use the standalone candidate. Vanilla demonstrates variant inheritance [2, Land Reform], and SB's policy records the limits on explicit child stances in the inspected build [1, POL-02].

### 4. Verify the two paths before choosing values

1. **ORL on a fresh start.** Check the enacted Land Reform law, separate military and slavery settings, incorporation status and applicable subsistence pasture method. Compare current Serfdom, Tenant Farmers and the candidate while controlling the economic setup.
2. **RHB at creation.** Record the enacted law immediately after the trek and after reload. Check the history-granted Homesteading setup as a comparator where applicable. Verify the alternative when RHB already exists and has reformed.
3. **Pasture, hiring and food.** Check the actual subsistence building selected for each state. For pasture, test explicit eligibility for `pm_serfdom_no`, an existing choice in the pasture method group [2, subsistence production methods and pasture method group]. Measure private ranch staffing, marketed goods, in-kind income and food security before and after incorporation. Keep food security and employment as separate success measures. The goal is a viable pastoral economy with household access and without the Serfdom employment or qualification restrictions.
