# Serfdom and customary land tenure in Zululand, Swaziland and Gaza

**Spes Bona research brief · 21 November 2026 · 1 January 1836 starts**

## Executive summary

**Prototype one Customary Land Tenure law for ZUL and SWZ. Include GZA in the comparison, with lower confidence in its territorial fit.** Set the proposed law's civilian subsistence protection to zero and use ordinary subsistence production and qualifications as the baseline. Test a modest arable reserve separately. Each tag currently inherits Vanilla Serfdom [1, country histories], [2, starting politics, lines 74–113].

Royal centres drew on organised service [3, pp. 41–43], [5, table 1 and “Structural layout of an ikhanda”]. Dingane also sought the return of future fugitives through a specific 1835 agreement [6, no. 91, arts. 1–3]. | **Customary Land Tenure.** Represent royal service and the Port Natal agreement through the institutions and parties that enforced them. |

## Introduction

This companion expands the three Nguni-related cases in the [Basotho-centred Serfdom brief](sb_customary_land_tenure_research_brief_2026_11_20.md). The decision is which land tenure and labour obligations belong in each tag’s national Land Reform law at the 1836 start. The scope is SB 0.20.3 and installed Victoria 3 1.15.2, with the project's CMF 1.67.9 compatibility patch [1, 1.15.2 implementation].

## Research approach

I compared the opening country and state blocks with the installed law, production-method and qualification scripts. For the historical fit I asked four questions. Who allocated land? Who normally worked it? Who could demand work or prevent departure? How far did that authority reach in 1836? Searches paired the three polities and their rulers with terms for land tenure, tribute, chiefly authority and service in English and Portuguese. I gave dated evidence the most weight on timing, used later research to interpret institutions, and checked where accounts relied on the same evidence. The direct starting-law assessment is static. The evidence on civilian transfers from the game's executable comes from an earlier build [11, “Confirmed native path”], [1, 1.15.2 runtime matrix, “Levy recruitment”].

## Findings

### Shared start in SB and Vanilla 1.15.2

The traditional starting helper activates **Serfdom** and **Closed Borders** [2, starting politics, lines 74–113]. The three SB history blocks call it and specify **Amabutho System**, **Subjecthood**, **Traditionalism** and **Land-Based Taxation** [1, country histories]. Each keeps the inherited Land Reform law [1, country histories], [2, starting politics, lines 74–113]. The slavery choices differ. ZUL and SWZ activate **Legacy Slavery** [1, ZUL and SWZ country histories], and GZA activates **Slave Trade** [1, GZA country history].

**Amabutho System** already gives conscription centres an exception to protected subsistence employment [1, Amabutho, lines 10–18], [2, modifier text, lines 2507–2508]. The authored ZUL start spans Zululand and a slice of Natal [1, state history, lines 6033–6045, 9200–9210], while SWZ occupies part of East Transvaal [1, state history, lines 9175–9187]. ### ZUL · Household production, royal service and restricted refuge

Guy describes land access as conditional on allegiance to the king [3, p. 34]. He identifies the homestead as the ordinary farming unit, with cultivation and cattle work organised through ranked households [3, pp. 34–40]. Kuper independently traces the extension of chiefly and household forms into the kingdom's political organisation [4, pp. 469–472, 476–480]. The accounts support household tenure under royal and chiefly authority. Kuper emphasises the continuity of chiefly household forms [4, pp. 469–472], while Guy traces the monarchy's capacity to extract labour through distinct royal institutions [3, pp. 40–43].

Guy’s detailed reconstruction chiefly concerns the 1870s [3, p. 33]. It distinguishes household production from agriculture and livestock work carried out for the king by regiments and others at royal homesteads [3, pp. 40–42]. He treats the king's control of regimental marriage as leverage over the creation of new homesteads [3, pp. 42–43]. Van der Merwe and Pikirayi identify Dingane's uMgungundlovu as a royal military homestead and describe its regimental and court spaces using archaeology alongside written accounts [5, table 1 and “Structural layout of an ikhanda”]. Estimates of the scale or regularity of obligatory field work across all his subjects remain much weaker.

In the 6 May 1835 treaty, Dingane pardoned people already at Port Natal who had fled his rule while still claiming them as subjects liable to be summoned [6, no. 91, art. 1]. Residents undertook to refuse future fugitives from his kingdom or its dependencies, to attempt to return them, and to report cases where return proved impracticable [6, no. 91, arts. 2–3]. The editorial epilogue to Fynn's diary describes the settlement's concern over Zulu refugees and Dingane's threats [12, pp. 238–240]. Its account of the negotiations relies on Gardiner [12, pp. 238–240 notes] and counts as context for the same episode. This is the strongest evidence of a specific movement restriction at the start. A general restriction on internal migration or civilian recruitment from every homestead would require broader evidence.

Confidence is high that Serfdom bundles too much into the land law. Confidence is moderate in the exact weight to give royal cultivation, military service and chiefly leverage in an opening model. Use conditional household tenure, a functioning Amabutho law, and a narrowly scoped interpretation of the treaty.

### SWZ · Uneven chiefly administration under Sobhuza

Bonner describes rebuilding after the Ndwandwe attacks through the incorporation of chiefdoms of varied origins [13, ch. 3 summary]. Gardiner recorded Ngwane envoys at Dingane's court in 1835 [14, pp. 167–171], [7, pp. 8–9]. Bonner compares that account with oral histories and identifies more extensive settlement in the south alongside a lightly settled and more militarised conquest zone [7, pp. 8–9]. He places several central appointments and effective occupations late in Sobhuza's reign or under Mswati [7, pp. 8–9]. Bonner disputes the envoys' picture of an exceptionally small male population and attributes part of the discrepancy to the setting in which the testimony was given [7, pp. 8–9].

In Hughes's twentieth-century study, *kubeka* refers to allocation of arable or building land to a person representing a group, usually a family [15, p. 258 n. 2]. Hughes documents a formal summons for tribute labour and work on fields attached to a chiefdom or royal centre [15, pp. 259–260 notes]. Rubin describes a process for departing one chief's area and seeking acceptance under another, including a possibility of refusal [16, pp. 93–94 notes]. The accounts describe conditional access and negotiated movement in a later period. Rubin cites Hughes on the narrower question of reoccupying land after departure [16, p. 94 n. 2]. The timing and prevalence of those procedures under Sobhuza remain open.

Bonner places the extension of royal villages, wider age-regiment organisation, and mobilisation of young men to work royal fields under Mswati [7, pp. 9–10]. The later evidence for tribute labour corroborates that such obligations existed [15, pp. 259–260 notes], with the chronology resting on Bonner's historical reconstruction. Harries also identifies Ngwane involvement in sales of captives around the Portuguese settlements [17, publisher extract]. Swazi military, slavery and land law can each represent the kind of obligation its evidence supports.

Confidence is moderate to high that SWZ needs a chiefly tenure law with weaker restrictions than Serfdom. Confidence is lower about the extent of civil labour duties in 1836. I would model households holding land through local authority in the starting law and reserve an increase in royal service for a later, evidenced programme of consolidation.

### GZA · A moving centre and varied local obligations

Santos reproduces António Nobre's 1833–34 account of intercepted messengers and negotiated deliveries of beads to Soshangane [8, pp. 78–79]. Her reconstruction also draws on military reports of an unsuccessful Portuguese campaign in 1834 [8, pp. 79–80]. Together they show the ability to impose payments and restrict passage on particular routes [8, pp. 78–80]. Liesegang places the Gaza centre in the Limpopo area through 1835 and north of the Save in 1836–38 [9, pp. 182–183]. Mathebula also places the settlement at Musapa in 1836 and draws in part on the same historical literature [10, “Migration of the Gaza into the vicinity of the Hlengwe”]. The evidence dates a transition within 1836 with precision too coarse to redraw the 1 January holdings.

Liesegang describes a stratified centre where captives could be incorporated into dominant households [9, pp. 178–179, 183–184]. He distinguishes subject chiefdoms that kept their own organisation and were attached to Nguni houses for taxation and administration [9, pp. 183–184]. Mathebula finds political subjection of the Sengwe branch among the south-eastern Zimbabwean Hlengwe and disputes extending that status to other branches [10, discussion of the Sengwe and Tshovani and conclusion]. Most of that evidence is later than Soshangane's initial expansion [10, discussion of the Sengwe and Tshovani]. Mathebula draws on Hlengwe accounts for the comparison between branches [10, discussion of the Sengwe and Tshovani] and cites Liesegang for the general structure of Gaza rule [10, discussion of the layers of Gaza political authority]. The resulting distinction is between coercion within the centre, obligations among subjects, and episodic coercion or tribute outside it.

Harries identifies participation by groups including the Gaza in the trade in captives [17, publisher extract]. Chewins and Delius corroborate substantial early trade at Delagoa Bay from Portuguese records and describe weaker polities near the bay as the main sources of captives [18, publisher abstract]. Harries dates the shift in incentives towards employing captives domestically to the 1860s [17, publisher extract]. Farré documents dispersed farming, women's participation in food sales and links to coastal markets across a broader southern Mozambique chronology [19, pp. 33–37]. Captive labour, local farming and tribute could coexist. Their extent in the SB-held slices on 1 January remains uncertain.

Confidence in replacing the uniform mobility and staffing restrictions is moderate. Confidence in a single detailed account of land allocation across the authored territories is lower. A starting law that recognises **local tenure** and limits its national effects to that common denominator is a better pilot. Use the slavery and military mechanics for those specific obligations. A later initiative concerned with tribute or court extraction should be based on where and when that obligation applied.

## Discussion and implications

A shared law can represent household cultivation under local authority while the scope of service and tribute varies by tag. In ZUL the evidence links royal service to particular institutions and refuge to a particular treaty [3, pp. 40–43], [5, table 1], [6, no. 91, arts. 1–3]. In SWZ it links the reach of royal labour to political consolidation over time [7, pp. 8–10], [15, pp. 259–260 notes]. In GZA it shows coercive relations whose intensity depends on position within or outside the core [9, pp. 178–179, 183–184], [10, conclusion]. The **civilian hiring floor** applies the same staffing rule across incorporated territory [2, Land Reform, line 56 and modifier text, lines 778–779].

The later Thirteen Chiefdoms settlement can apply `sb_zululand_chiefdom_reserves` to Zululand [1, Zululand settlement effects, lines 679–699]. That modifier adds 80 percentage points of protected subsistence employment, 15 percentage points of reserved arable land and +25% subsistence output [1, static modifiers, lines 735–741]. Test the later reserve on its own and after each starting-law comparison to see how much protection applies in combination.

## Limitations and evidence gaps

- **Zulu mobility.** The strongest printed evidence concerns a particular refuge agreement. It establishes the terms and reserved recall right [6, no. 91, arts. 1–3], with actual enforcement and internal relations beyond the agreement less well established. The material evidence for a royal centre measures physical organisation [5, “Structural layout of an ikhanda”], leaving the number of labourers and frequency of compulsory work uncertain.
- **Swazi back-projection.** The clearest accounts of named land practices and transfers are twentieth century [15, pp. 258–260 notes], [16, pp. 93–94 notes]. The breakdown of local tenure, cultivation and royal obligations within SB's East Transvaal slice requires finer 1830s evidence.
- **Gaza geography.** Liesegang's detailed institutional reconstruction is principally for 1840–1895 [9, pp. 178–182]. Santos is closer to the start and chiefly establishes particular demands and control of routes [8, pp. 78–80]. The evidence is too coarse to fix a frontier, a labour share or uniform ownership terms for the game map.
- **Gameplay.** The current opening law and its script effects were checked statically. The native civilian transfer evidence is for 1.14.5 [11, “Confirmed native path”]. Fresh 1.15.2 staffing, production-method choice, migration and reform outcomes require campaign checks [1, 1.15.2 implementation and runtime matrix]. Every trial number below is a balance choice.

## Recommendations

### 1. Use an independent Customary Land Tenure law

Give it an ordinary subsistence baseline, zero additional civilian staffing protection, typical farmer qualifications, and omit the mechanical bans on peasant movement in the Land Reform group. Use **zero additional arable reserve as the first comparison**, then test **+25 percentage points in incorporated states** if a reserve is needed for balance. Tenant Farmers supplies an existing calibration point for that figure [2, Land Reform, lines 141–160]. Begin without a new Landowner or Farmer political strength modifier. Hold the Army Model constant to control for the political strength effects of the Amabutho law [1, Amabutho, lines 10–18]. Apply the candidate after the traditional starting helper in each trial, since that helper supplies the initial Serfdom law [2, starting politics, lines 74–113].

**Suggested English description.** “Households cultivate land under local custom. Chiefs and other recognised authorities allocate land and uphold obligations attached to its use.” For **ZUL**, tie any future refugee agreement or court-service event to the authority that can enforce it. For **SWZ**, make increased royal mobilisation a possible consequence of Mswati's consolidation. For **GZA**, use wording that recognises different local rules and test the existing holdings before adopting the shared model.

A standalone law needs explicit interest-group and movement stances. Test conservative support for chiefly allocation alongside demand for a move to private smallholdings. SB's implementation notes warn that variants inherit their parent's stances [1, POL-02]. The installed game also uses family checks for qualifications [2, farmers, lines 94–102 and triggers, lines 1156–1158]. Explicitly qualify the new law for an ordinary subsistence production method. The Vanilla `pm_serfdom_no` enumerates eligible laws, is present in both the farm and orchard groups, and uses an in-kind coefficient of 4 [2, subsistence production methods, lines 48–63 and production method groups, lines 16–49]. Validate production and in-kind income at actual staffing in both building types before choosing method or output coefficients for release.

### 2. Use Vanilla laws and amendments where their scope fits

| Option | Fit for these starts |
| --- | --- |
| **Tenant Farmers** | Use as a temporary comparator. It has the 25-point incorporated arable reserve, +25% Landowners strength and an internal peasant migration ban but no active protected staffing modifier [2, Land Reform, lines 141–160]. Its hereditary landlord model [2, English law descriptions, lines 989–990] is a poor fit for household and local allocation here. |
| **Tenant Farmers variant** | Use only if the inherited stances suit the intended politics. Vanilla's own Tenant Farmers variants declare the parent and their own modifiers [2, Land Reform, lines 239–276, 295–324]. Check family-specific effects and method eligibility before taking this route [1, POL-02], [2, subsistence production methods, lines 48–63]. |
| **Manorialism** | Its removal of the specific Serfdom migration flags is useful evidence that modifiers can differ within a family [2, Land Reform, lines 51–57, 74–124]. It retains 50-point civilian staffing protection, remains in the Serfdom qualifications family and supplies a corvée method [2, Land Reform, lines 74–124, farmers, lines 94–102 and subsistence production methods, lines 65–83]. It fits a much stronger landlord-labour claim than the evidence supports. |
| **Peasant Proprietorship** | Use as a distinct reform option. The law gives farmers political strength and allows partial worker ownership [2, Land Reform, lines 420–447]. Its independent-proprietor framing fits a move towards individual ownership [2, English law descriptions, lines 1003–1004]. |
| **Starting amendment on a Vanilla host** | An amendment can attach local effects to a host but adds modifiers to it [2, amendment schema, lines 1–11]. Gates of the Bosphorus attaches a Çiftlik amendment to Serfdom [20, Çiftlik amendment], and SB's Shepstone System uses an explicit host list with its own stance parent [1, Shepstone System amendment]. Use amendments for a documented additional obligation where a host is already appropriate. That is a better fit for later or optional content. |
| **Hail Columbia's Land Stewardship pattern** | Use its Land Reform presentation as a precedent. It is a Collectivised Agriculture variant with high worker-ownership allowances and economic modifiers [21, law and English localisation]. Those mechanical choices suit a different tenure model. |

### 3. Compare the starts in a matched 1.15.2 run

1. **Open each tag in January and verify the authored setup.** Compare inherited Serfdom, Tenant Farmers and the standalone prototype. Check country law, active subsistence PM, state incorporation, arable reserve and staffing protection in each holding. Include Zambezia as the explicit unincorporated check [1, state history, lines 3476–3482].
2. **Measure the labour and food effects.** Offer the same viable civilian vacancy within each tag's matched runs and record hires out of subsistence, farmer qualifications, wages, subsistence staffing, market grain or fruit and household in-kind income. Follow the first month and first year's harvest and hiring outcomes. Use a branch that offsets only the 50-point floor in an incorporated state if the law-to-law comparison leaves the cause of a recruitment difference unclear [11, “State scope” and “Narrow proposal and limits”]. Test internal migration separately with a valid destination. Use independent conscription runs and the existing levy matrix for recruitment [1, 1.15.2 runtime matrix, “Levy recruitment”].
3. **Check the reform and formation paths.** Verify that alternative laws remain enactable, that AI and movements treat the new law as intended, and that the active law and production methods survive each eligible path to the Nguni formable. ZUL, SWZ and GZA are eligible tags in the formation decision and share its inheritance helper [1, Nguni formation decision and inheritance effect]. Include the later Zululand reserve branch for the ZUL comparison [1, Zululand settlement effects, lines 679–699]. Use an incorporated state when measuring the effects of the floor. A successful pilot permits civilian hiring when applicants are eligible, uses a valid method at startup and after reform, and preserves the chosen interpretation of tenure within each test case.
