# Serfdom and customary land tenure in Spes Bona

20 November 2026 · Research brief for the 1836 balance review

## Executive summary

**Pilot a standalone Customary Land Tenure law for Basotho (BST).** Spes Bona's BST, SWZ, ZUL and GZA country histories call the traditional starting-politics helper [1, country history], which activates Vanilla Serfdom [2, `00_political_setup.txt`]. That law bans internal and mass peasant migration [2, `01_land_reform.txt` and modifier descriptions], raises the minimum share of incorporated arable land reserved for subsistence [2, `01_land_reform.txt` and modifier descriptions], and shields workers from civilian recruitment at subsistence buildings with low staffing [2, `01_land_reform.txt` and modifier descriptions].

In nineteenth-century Basotho practice, chiefs allocated fields to households [3, p. 159], [4, p. 265] and regulated access to shared pasture [3, pp. 159–160], [4, p. 265], [5, “The Traditional Land Tenure Arrangement”]. Households could leave a chief's jurisdiction and seek land elsewhere [6, p. 172], [7, pp. 57–58], surrendering their existing allotment when they left [3, pp. 159–160], [6, p. 172]. Chiefs could call for labour [3, pp. 162–163], [4, p. 265], and some people were held in much more coercive personal bonds [8, pp. 287–288, 304–307], [9, pp. 44–45]. The evidence for conditional tenure and unequal obligations is strong. Start by modelling household access to land and leave civilian job changes open. Confidence in the proposed gameplay values and their fit for other tags is lower.

In the 1.14.5 mountain-retreat save, incorporated Lesotho's subsistence farm was at about 11% staffing [10, 1 Nov. snapshot], [11, “Confirmed native path”]. Its 50% employment floor blocked transfers of protected workers to other workplaces under the archived engine rules [10, 1 Nov. snapshot], [11, “Confirmed native path”]. Use this scenario to test the new law before widening the rollout.

## Introduction

The decision is which rights and obligations the Land Reform law should represent on **1 January 1836**. The core scope follows the SB balance register and its comparison of Basotho (BST), Swazi (SWZ), Zulu (ZUL) and Gaza (GZA) [1, balance register and four-tag audit]. The neighbouring histories and successor tags are a rollout check. Later-century sources help reconstruct earlier practices and identify changes after the start date. The technical target is SB 0.20.3 on Victoria 3 1.15.2 with all DLC and SB's patched Community Mod Framework [1, 1.15.2 implementation].

The test asks who allocated land, what households owed for access, and what happened when they moved or took different work. In this brief, **customary tenure** means rules of allocation and use recognised by the polity or local community. **Serfdom** means the installed package of land, migration and employment modifiers [2, `01_land_reform.txt`].

## Research approach

This targeted review and claim audit used scholarly bibliographic records in AfricaBib and JSTOR, university and library collections, and publisher texts. Searches combined polity names with land allocation, grazing, labour tribute, and the terms *mafisa*, *bohlanka* and *matsema*. The audit followed citations to early observers and checked them against later oral histories, fieldwork or archival studies. The audit counts repeated use of the same testimony once. Evidence from later periods corroborates the existence of an institution more readily than its reach in 1836. References identify where only an extract or abstract was used.

The technical audit checked the installed 1.15.2 files [2], SB history and current relief scripts [1], the archived 1.14.5 hiring investigation [11], and the retained November 1841 save against its extraction and hash [10, 1 Nov. snapshot]. The comparison uses the settings in the installed scripts and a historical save from the older engine. The proposed 1.15.2 comparison still needs a matched replay of the retreat.

## Findings

### Basotho land and household rights

Casalis describes senior chiefs assigning territory and subordinate authorities allocating fields to family heads [3, p. 159]. The allocation gave cultivators continued use while they remained in the locality [3, pp. 159–160], [6, p. 172]. The ordinary allottee was a male household head in Casalis's account [3, p. 159], and later reconstructions describe allocation to married men [5, “The Traditional Land Tenure Arrangement”], [7, pp. 57, 60–61]. Women were major agricultural producers [3, p. 159], [12, abstract]. The allocation model should represent household access without implying equal individual title.

Families could change allegiance and seek another chief [6, p. 172], [7, pp. 57–59]. They surrendered the fields they had held under their former chief [3, pp. 159–160], [6, p. 172], who could reallocate them [3, p. 159]. How often families left and who could afford to do so in 1836 remain uncertain.

Household cultivation coexisted with shared grazing [4, p. 265], [7, pp. 60–62]. Access to pasture was regulated [3, pp. 159–160], [5, “The Traditional Land Tenure Arrangement”], including winter reserves described by Casalis [3, pp. 159–160] and later attested in the evidence surveyed by Conz [7, pp. 61–62]. Thabane distinguishes chiefly allocation authority from cultivators' use rights [5, “The Traditional Land Tenure Arrangement”]. His later dispute evidence shows that chiefs and users could put competing claims on the same resource [5, “The Traditional Land Tenure Arrangement” and “Chiefly Land Disputes”]. The existence of an allotment rule provides no guarantee of secure or equal access.

### Labour, cattle and chiefly extraction

Casalis describes reciprocal work parties between households [3, pp. 162–163], and Conz records later interview evidence for small *matsema* on household fields [7, pp. 63–64]. Chiefs called work parties to cultivate chiefly fields [3, pp. 162–163], [4, p. 265], [9, pp. 44–46]. Some of the produce provisioned people who depended on the chief [7, pp. 62–63], [9, pp. 44–45], including refugees and orphans in Maliehe's account [9, pp. 44–45]. Some chiefs used the resulting labour to produce crops for sale [9, pp. 44–46], [7, p. 63]. Maliehe cites Thabane for chiefly exactions [9, pp. 44–46]. Conz also identifies complaints about sale and exploitation in his account of late-century *letsema* [7, p. 63].

Under *mafisa*, patrons lent cattle while keeping ownership [3, pp. 154–155], [7, pp. 58–60], [9, pp. 44–45]. Clients obtained benefits such as milk and supplied care for the cattle [3, pp. 154–155], [7, pp. 58–60]. Moshoeshoe could use loans to support poorer households and secure their allegiance [7, pp. 58–60], [9, pp. 44–45]. Cattle loans were one source of dependence. In some cases, chiefly provision of bridewealth led to a much stronger bond of *bohlanka* and claims over children or labour [8, pp. 287–288, 304–307], [9, pp. 44–45]. Morelli's reconstruction of an October 1835 case records Moshoeshoe claiming ‘Maklobo's child because he had supplied bridewealth for the marriage [8, pp. 304–305]. That case speaks directly to the opening period and concerns a specific bonded family.

Eldredge emphasises how the search for security shaped mutual obligations and how access to resources changed over time [13, Introduction]. Thabane foregrounds exactions linked to land and allegiance [5, “Chiefly Land Disputes”], discussing a period that some historians have called feudal [5, “Chiefly Land Disputes”]. Both lenses accommodate unequal power. The grounds for a Serfdom analogy are conditional land access and compulsory labour [3, pp. 159–163], [5, “Chiefly Land Disputes”], [4, p. 265]. A blanket movement or employment penalty goes beyond what those obligations establish, given the evidence that households could leave a chief and surrender the allotment [3, pp. 159–160], [6, p. 172] and that some bonded families had a distinct status [8, pp. 287–288, 304–307], [9, pp. 44–45].

### Change over the nineteenth century

Mid-century wars with the Boers reduced Basotho access to arable land [13, Introduction], [7, pp. 69–70], and the resulting territorial limits compounded pressure on grazing and fields later in the century [13, Introduction], [7, pp. 60–62]. Casalis reports a ready market for Basotho grain [3, pp. 169–170]. Maliehe traces grain sales and early wage activity [9, pp. 46–50], while Eldredge documents later grain sales and wage work at Kimberley [13, Introduction]. The evidence supports household cultivation alongside market participation. Maliehe uses Casalis for part of the trade evidence and also draws on work on commodity production and wage labour [9, pp. 45–50]. The first written Laws of Lerotholi date to 1903 [14, statement of objects and reasons], [15, extract]. Use that codification as a later reference point for adjudication and recording rights.

## Discussion and implications

Separate access to arable land from access to another job. The reservation sets how much incorporated arable land remains available for subsistence [2, modifier descriptions]. The protected-employment modifier controls whether the occupants can be recruited away at a given staffing level [2, modifier descriptions], [11, “Confirmed native path”]. A modest reservation can preserve household cultivation in the proposed law. Its value is a balance question.

Treat marketed grain, in-kind subsistence income and hiring as separate outcomes. The two ordinary subsistence production methods apply different grain and income effects [2, `12_subsistence.txt`]. In the present SB source, the Mountain Refuge grant lasts ten years [1, BST retreat event] and grants a +30% modifier to in-kind subsistence income [1, BST retreat modifier], [2, in-kind income definition]. The earlier throughput bonus is commented out in the current definition [1, BST retreat modifier]. Hold the current relief fixed across law trials. Keep Closed Borders as a separate control because it has its own migration restrictiveness modifier [2, `02_migration.txt`].

## Limitations and evidence gaps

- **Basotho evidence.** Casalis observed early Basotho society but sometimes generalised across several southern African peoples [3, pp. 154–160]. Ellenberger's compilation combines longer missionary experience with oral reconstruction [4, foreword and preface]. Hamnett's legal fieldwork dates from 1964 and 1966 [6, p. 17], and Conz uses some of the same missionary accounts for cattle patronage [7, pp. 58–60] and draws on Basutoland National Council records for switching allegiance [7, pp. 57–58]. The convergence supports the institutions described here. It gives little basis for estimating the frequency of departure, the share of bonded households or the amount of required labour in 1836.
- **Other tags.** Bonner treats elements of the Swazi analysis as provisional [16, p. 1] and Hughes's field evidence comes from a later political setting [17, extract and résumé]. Guy models the Zulu economy of the 1870s [18, pp. 33–34], and Mathebula's research tests relationships with a particular frontier population [22, pp. 1, 4–7]. Confidence in precise launch conditions and in a shared law is lower than for BST.
- **Version boundary.** The raw save establishes staffing in the archived 1.14.5 case [10, 1 Nov. snapshot] and the hiring code explains the rule that prevented protected workers from transferring [11, “Confirmed native path”]. The installed 1.15.2 scripts establish current inputs [2, `01_land_reform.txt`]. How quickly a new law changes employment, food security or political support remains a playtest question.

## Recommendations

### 1. Pilot a standalone Customary Land Tenure law for BST

Select it in BST history after the starting-politics helper, which currently grants Serfdom [1, BST history], [2, `00_political_setup.txt`]. Model chiefly allocation of household fields and regulation of shared pasture [3, pp. 159–160], [4, p. 265], with use conditional on residence and obligations [6, p. 172], [5, “The Traditional Land Tenure Arrangement”].

| Design choice | First trial |
| --- | --- |
| **Household land access** | Trial a **25%** minimum incorporated subsistence share, using the Tenant Farmers modifier as the numerical reference [2, `01_land_reform.txt`]. Measure whether the share leaves viable household agriculture and room for a farm or ranch in small state portions. |
| **Employment and movement** | Give the candidate zero protected employment from the base law and leave both peasant migration flags unset. Compare hiring with the 50% Serfdom floor [2, `01_land_reform.txt`], [11, “Confirmed native path”]. Hold the separate Migration law constant. |
| **Production and politics** | Trial explicit eligibility for `pm_serfdom_no`, which uses a higher in-kind income coefficient and supplies no separate grain bonus [2, `12_subsistence.txt`]. Audit the other subsistence building types used by BST. Give the candidate appropriate SB ideology stances, and measure the effect of giving up Serfdom's +25% Landowners modifier [2, `01_land_reform.txt`]. |
| **Applicability** | Verify the new start, BST's route to STA [1, Sotho-Tswana formation], ordinary reforms and subject-law changes. Scope initial availability to the pilot and the relevant successor path. |

Vanilla Manorialism and Latifundias carry their own modifiers [2, `01_land_reform.txt`] while taking political stances from their parent laws [2, `01_land_reform.txt`], [1, POL-02]. Serfdom variants also match the native qualification trigger [2, `farmers.txt` and `00_scripted_triggers.txt`]. A standalone law gives SB distinct political choices for customary allocation, Serfdom and freehold. It requires checking production eligibility and reviewing law checks and AI weights.

### 2. Use an amendment for chiefly work parties if the pilot needs a distinct political dispute

A **Chiefly Work Parties** amendment could represent obligatory service, which appears alongside reciprocal work in the historical accounts [3, pp. 162–163], [4, p. 265], [9, pp. 44–46]. Let the interest group representing the chiefs sponsor the amendment and test a modest political-strength effect if one is needed. Use BST's existing Chiefly Levy army law for military mobilisation [1, BST history and Chiefly Levy law]. Keep the household access rules in the base law so the work obligation can be repealed separately.

Gates of the Bosphorus makes its Çiftlik amendment available to TUR and attaches it to Serfdom [24, `amendment_gbbf_chiftlik`]. SB's Shepstone System uses `allowed_laws` for permissible hosts and `parent` for the law from which political stances are drawn [1, Shepstone amendment], [2, amendment schema]. Hail Columbia's **Land Stewardship** is a named Collectivised Agriculture variant [25, law and English localisation] and raises worker-ownership limits [25, law]. Its name and variant structure are useful design references. The installed amendment schema adds modifiers to the host law [2, amendment schema]. Use the base law to set the desired movement flags and employment floor.

| **Commercialised / Collectivised Agriculture** | Use them for distinct ownership reforms, with investment by financial districts or collective worker ownership respectively [2, `01_land_reform.txt` and English law descriptions]. | Basotho producers participated in grain markets under customary tenure [3, pp. 159, 169–170], [9, pp. 46–50]. Shared grazing could coexist with cultivation by individual households [4, p. 265], [7, pp. 60–62]. |

### 4. Verify BST before applying the model elsewhere

Compare the present setup, Vanilla Tenant Farmers and the standalone candidate from matched fresh starts. Replay the mountain retreat from before the transfer using the present Mountain Refuge setup [1, BST retreat event and modifier]. Track reserved arable land, eligible production methods, qualified applicants, maize and ranch staffing, marketed grain, in-kind income, wages, food security and conscription. Check ideology and movement support, AI enactment, economic-law prerequisites and the STA successor path [1, Sotho-Tswana formation]. The pilot succeeds if eligible workers can take vacant jobs and household agriculture remains viable at the start and after the retreat. Review SWZ and ZUL against finer evidence for the opening period, and distinguish Gaza's core from its tributary periphery before choosing a nationwide tenure law.
