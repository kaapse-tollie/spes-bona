# Fixed historical Zulu houses and political followings, 1836–1900

**Research date:** 9 November 2026  
**Purpose:** Historical foundations for SB's persistent court and Thirteen Chiefdoms design.  
**Status:** Researched design advice; no scripts, localisation or gameplay changed.

## Executive summary

**Use a finite catalogue of historical identities, with different kinds of membership and leadership. Do not turn the present fourteen followings into fourteen equivalent hereditary houses.** Guy's reconstruction separates chiefly constituencies, royal establishments and royal branches; Hamilton demonstrates that even a seemingly straightforward royal genealogy can be contested. [1, pp. 56–65], [2, pp. 219–224]

For **1836**, the strongest opening candidates are **abaQulusi under Mkabayi**, **Ndlela's Ntuli following**, and **Maphitha's house**, with the later label **Mandlakazi** available as its history develops. **Ntonteleni under Mawa** and **esiKlebheni under Langazana** are defensible royal establishments, with an explicit qualification that their exact 1836 incumbencies are reconstructed rather than established by an inspected appointment record. **Buthelezi, Mdlalose and amaCube** are credible continuing identities whose opening headship needs greater care. The evidence does not justify opening all later leaders as established chiefs. The full audit below distinguishes these recommendations from documented dates.

The principal later additions should be the **Cetshwayo and Mbuyazi house groupings**, **Hamu's house of Nzibe/Ngenetsheni**, and, conditionally, **Shingana's Onkweni establishment**. Mpande's son's testimony distinguishes house membership from a simple list of biological siblings. Dinuzulu should normally continue Cetshwayo's branch; the broader **uSuthu** allegiance should be separate. **Mthonga** warrants a historical royal actor and claimant, but the evidence inspected here does not establish a comparably durable independent constituency. [3, pp. 102–104]

**A persistent recognised head is reasonable; an irremovable head until death or vacancy is not.** Ntulizwe's testimony disputes a colonial successor's legitimacy and describes an earlier caretaker appointment, while research on Dunn's domain finds fragmentation after his death. These are materially different succession problems. [4, pp. 206–209], [5, pp. 6–9]

The recommended minimum is therefore: stable identity; explicit historical membership; one current recognised head or acting representative; separately recorded rival claimants, coalition alignment and territorial office; and no duplicated backing from counting a branch inside its parent dynasty and again on its own. There is no historical reason to force this catalogue to contain exactly thirteen entries.

## Introduction: what is being fixed?

The question is which named identities can remain intelligible across the opening court, the succession conflicts, and the partition/restoration period. The unit must survive a change of character without implying that its supporters or territory remain constant.

This brief uses **house** narrowly when discussing genealogy and more cautiously when proposing interface categories. Kuper treats the house, its ranked divisions and segmentation as central to Zulu political organisation. Only his publisher's extract and exposed notes were accessible here, so this brief does not claim to have reviewed the full argument. Hanretta's account of women's institutions also warns against reducing authority to a succession of male chiefs. [6, extract and n. 51], [7, pp. 398–399]

| Entity | What may persist | What must not be inferred automatically |
|---|---|---|
| Clan or named descent group | A recognised affiliation and historical name | One chief, one territory or unanimous political allegiance |
| Royal genealogical house or branch | A recognised line, ranked house affiliation, inheritance claims | Identical biological parentage for every member; automatic political loyalty |
| Royal homestead or establishment | A named centre, dependants, office and memory | A separate male hereditary dynasty |
| Chiefly political following | A leader's clients and local authority | That every follower shares the leader's descent identity |
| Regimental constituency | Service associations and recruitment connections | Ownership by one house or permanent alignment in a succession dispute |
| Colonial chiefdom | Jurisdiction conferred or recognised at a particular date | An ancient house of the same size or an unbroken traditional succession |
| Coalition | A temporary or continuing political cause | An additional population to add to the constituent houses' backing |

These are analytical distinctions used for the design; a historical name can refer to more than one category in different contexts. **Earliest safe availability** below means a supported modelling window, not a claimed foundation date. “1836, qualified” identifies a reasonable opening reconstruction whose exact-year documentation remains incomplete. “Not established” means the research did not establish it, not that the person or following did not exist.

The present implementation was checked as a hypothesis list: positive following IDs 1–14 in `sb_court_custom_loc.txt` and `sb_court_politics_l_english.yml`; individual following ID 0; and the ruler-dependent Royal Household handling in `sb_court_politics_effects.txt`. The prior coalition and oral-history briefs and the 9 November design discussion supplied leads, not independent historical corroboration.

## Research approach

This is a **targeted historical evidence review**, conducted on 9 November 2026, covering Zululand and relevant Natal/Transvaal exile connections, principally 1836–1900. Later testimony is used retrospectively and identified as such. English-language scholarship and edited/translated testimony predominate; isiZulu names and variant spellings were searched together.

Discovery used two substantive routes: **UCT's eMandulo/Five Hundred Year Archive and university thesis repositories** for testimony and full texts; and **Cambridge Core journal/publisher searching**, supplemented by AfricaBib, Google Books, CiNii and institutional catalogue records for scholarly discovery and identifier checking. Backward searches followed Guy, Hamilton and the existing briefs into named Stuart interlocutors. Forward searches included the 2026 Onkweni article. Representative queries combined the names with “house”, “succession”, “Nzibe”, “Mandlakazi”, “Ngenetsheni”, “chief”, and spelling variants such as Shingana/Tshingana and Sigananda/Sig(a)nanda. This was not an exhaustive search of JSTOR, Scopus, Web of Science or archival holdings.

Lawful full texts were obtained from institutional repositories, publisher open access, the Anglo-Zulu War Historical Society and Project Gutenberg. No paywall was bypassed. Kuper and Ballard are used only to the extent supported by their accessible publisher extracts/notes. Page references refer to inspected printed pages, except where a PDF-page reference is explicitly stated. The Hughes copy is the author's reformatted version, whose pagination differs from the original thesis.

The **James Stuart Archive (JSA)** is indispensable primary evidence, but consists of later conversations selected, recorded and sometimes translated by Stuart, then critically edited. A participant's memories, inherited tradition, an editor's correction, and a modern historian's inference are identified separately where the distinction affects a decision. Multiple authors quoting the same testimony are not independent witnesses. Colonial records cited within theses were not themselves inspected unless stated; their file references below are retrieval leads.

### Corrections that matter before implementation

**Mandlakazi genealogy is a dispute, not a missing lookup.** Hamilton examines conflicting accounts of Ngwabi, Mhlaba and Sojiyisa, including leviratic affiliation and denials of a biological royal connection. Choosing one biological chain without qualification would turn politically interested tradition into a settled family tree. The durable identity is Maphitha's established following and its successors. [2, pp. 219–223]

**Hamu's earlier appearance needs a precise caveat.** Shamase places conflict over Hamu's growing support in late June **1857**. Adjacent notes cite a Rathbone letter of 26 June and, inconsistently, 26 January, enclosed in correspondence dated 30 June. The thesis supports an 1857 window, not an exact creation date for Ngenetsheni. The underlying letter should be checked. Cope independently establishes Hamu as the intended beneficiary of Pretorius's **June 1865** intervention scheme. [15, pp. 184–185, nn. 113–115], [16, p. 39]

**Ntuli succession is not a clean list of blood heirs.** Ntulizwe's 1920 account is exceptionally relevant because he explains how a return to ancestral homesteads could attract followers away from the recognised chief. His testimony is an interested claimant's account; it proves that competing authority and remembered inheritance mattered, not that every allegation is adjudicated fact. Preserve **recognised incumbent**, **acting caretaker** and **dynastic claimant** as distinguishable states. [4, pp. 206–209]

**Name similarity must not create family or political identity.** Shingana's editors warn that **Nhlaka kaDikane of the Mdlalose** is confused with **Mdlaka kaNcidi of emGazini**. Hamilton distinguishes **eGazini** from **emGazini**. Royal testimony distinguishes **Siteku** from **Sikhotha/Sikota**. These are useful identity checks for the cast, not grounds for an opportunistic spelling merge. [21, p. 245, n. 11], [2, p. 214], [3, pp. 102–104]

**Even a maternal link can be expressed differently by different sources.** Cope calls Mkhungo and Sikhotha Monase's sons, whereas Mgidhlana places **Sikota under Masala**. The latter is more direct family testimony, but neither wording should be silently rewritten to make every allied prince a biological member of Monase's house. Record the testimony and preserve the wider political association. [16, p. 27], [3, p. 104]

**A new publication is not automatically the strongest source.** Nxumalo's accessible 2026 article offers Onkweni archival leads but includes an impossible statement placing a killing by Cetshwayo in **1888**, after Cetshwayo's death. Its exile/banishment chronology also needs comparison with the JSA editorial chronology. Use it as a map to the cited files, with individual claims checked; do not implement its entire succession narrative by authority of the journal alone. [24], [13, p. 169, n. 14], [21, p. 245, n. 1]

## Discussion: persistent heads, spokespeople and independent backing

### A stable head is defensible with exceptions

The best historical abstraction is **a recognised head who persists until a meaningful succession or political change**. Re-ranking the head whenever an unrelated character gains court prominence would conflate influence with inheritance or recognised authority. Equally, holding the head forever until death would exclude removal, exile, repudiation, a rival's recognition and the fragmentation of a following.

The minimum useful distinctions are:

1. **Head or claimant by house succession** — appropriate where the actual line and its rules are attested.
2. **Acting head or regent** — exemplified by emGazini; the acting adult and the minor heir must not both receive the same following's full backing.
3. **Holder of a royal establishment** — appropriate for abaQulusi, Ntonteleni and esiKlebheni; no hereditary male succession should be invented to fill a vacancy.
4. **Political spokesperson** — the person presently speaking for an otherwise stable identity in court. This can differ from an absent, minor or excluded head.
5. **Recognised colonial chief** — an office that may strengthen an existing leader, detach a section or place a new intermediary over people whose earlier affiliations continue.

These are design recommendations derived from the different cases above, not five universal indigenous constitutional offices. A game can retain one visible representative while recording the exceptional situation in context. An unknown historical succession may remain vacant or be handled as a clearly authored alternate-history appointment; it must not be presented as a recovered historical fact.

### Preventing double-counting without erasing affiliations

Keep **one exclusive accounting home for each contribution of backing**, while allowing several descriptive relationships. A character can be Hamu's biological brother, an uThulwana veteran, a member of a local house and a supporter of a royal claimant; those are not four independent constituencies.

| Case | Relationship worth retaining | Backing to count once |
|---|---|---|
| Hamu | Biological link to Mpande; house of Nzibe; changing coalition | His independently established following |
| Cetshwayo and Dinuzulu | Father–son continuity, wider royal kin, uSuthu allegiance | The branch's direct base, plus separately identified allies only once |
| Zibhebhu and Hayiyana | Shared Mandlakazi history; rival house divisions and later alignment | Either a shared, divided total or distinct demonstrable sections; never the full total for each |
| Mkabayi and a Mdlalose official | Royal custodianship and the official's descent identity | The establishment's actual dependants, separate from any demonstrated other local constituency |
| Mnyamana | House connection, military command, chief-councillor office | His local/personal following; offices may alter influence without recreating those supporters |
| Dunn after 1895 | Surviving family and former headmen's sections | The surviving sections, without adding Dunn's former entire domain on top |

This is also the answer to native generated characters: **a generated person is not a new historical house, and surname agreement is not membership evidence**. Use an unaffiliated personal following unless membership is explicitly authored or established through an in-game relationship. A generated successor may represent alternate history, but should not acquire a fabricated historical pedigree.

### Additions, mergers and exclusions

**Add or separate first:** the Cetshwayo and Mbuyazi branches; Hamu's Nzibe affiliation; the distinction between an incumbent royal establishment and its holder's family. Keep Gqugqu and Mthonga as historically distinct actors without guaranteeing them a permanent territorial constituency. Research Onkweni before giving it an unqualified full succession chain.

**Do not merge:** Ncwana and Nyandu solely because they are Qwabe; Ngenetsheni into Mpande's family bloc; all Ntuli sections into one unanimous side; eGazini and emGazini; or abaQulusi into Mdlalose. **Do not split merely by generation:** Dinuzulu from Cetshwayo's branch. The relevant test is independent political organisation, not the arrival of a new adult surname.

**Demote from automatic great-house status:** Royal Household as a hereditary identity; Komfiya's Ncwana connection until a bounded following is established; Nongalaza's following as an eternal hereditary unit without a successor; and Dunn's undivided domain after his death. This need not remove the people from court.

**Further historical additions, without filling a quota:** Guy identifies **Ntshingwayo's Khoza**, **Lukwazi/Ndabankulu's Ntombela**, and other significant local constituencies. Shingana's testimony independently recalls **Lukwazi's** importance across Shaka, Dingane and Mpande's reigns. Ntombela is consequently a particularly useful next opening-house investigation. **Mahanana's Mphangisweni** is a later royal-establishment lead. These names warrant targeted succession research, not immediate conversion into complete 1836 rosters. [1, pp. 61, 64], [21, p. 243]

The **Thirteen Chiefdoms** should be treated as the result of a dated colonial settlement, capable of cutting across earlier affiliations. The historical house catalogue can contain fewer or more entries. A settlement recipient, a local head, a royal claimant and an old house need not all be the same entity.

## Limitations and targeted evidence gaps

This review establishes a defensible architecture and a prioritised historical cast; it does **not** establish every leader's lifetime, exact accession date or complete descendant list. No numerical follower counts or personality traits are recommended. Several dates in older literature are inferred retrospectively, and accounts that draw on the same JSA witness must not be treated as independent confirmation.

| Priority | Unresolved question | Best next retrieval and what it would decide |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Who actually headed the opening Buthelezi, Mdlalose and amaCube followings in January 1836? | Check contemporary visitors' records and the named families' additional JSA testimony against later genealogies. This decides the opening representative, not whether the identity existed. |
| **1** | Is Ngenetsheni's independent political base already securely dated in 1857? | Retrieve the Rathbone-to-Williams letter and Williams-to-Shepstone enclosure cited by Shamase, **30 June 1857**, comparing the conflicting June/January date in the notes. Do not silently invent a manuscript reference from the thesis's noisy scan. |
| **1** | What are the competing Ntuli lines and recognised offices after Godide? | Check the provincial records behind Ndhlovu and the magistrate/high-court proceedings mentioned by Ntulizwe. Verify **Mavumengwana–Zinti–Ndlela** before writing biological relationships. |
| **1** | Which parts of Mandlakazi followed Hayiyana, Zibhebhu and later claimants? | Pair Socwatsha's inheritance account with civil-war testimony and appointment records. This decides whether to model one divided identity or two independently weighted sections. |
| **2** | Who succeeded the female holders of Ntonteleni and esiKlebheni, if the establishments survived in comparable form? | Search by establishment name as well as incumbent. Absence of a known successor should remain an explicit gap. |
| **2** | Did Komfiya and Nongalaza have independently inherited territorial followings? | Resolve migration/residency and named heirs before promoting them from political connections or personal followings to perpetual houses. |
| **2** | Who was emGazini's minor heir, and when did Sitshaluza's regency end? | Follow Guy's source trail and family testimony. Do not equate **Mabhoko**, the eldest/influential son, with the unnamed designated heir without evidence. |
| **2** | How did Dunn's household and headmen's sections divide authority in 1895–1900? | Mmutlana points to later succession disputes, including **SNA 1/1/377, 1907/2638–2639**. Inspect original records before assigning a successor or an exact subdivision date. These files were not opened for this brief. |
| **2** | What is the securely dated Onkweni sequence? | Retrieve the specific colonial correspondence cited by Nxumalo for **Ngobozana kaVukuza** and **Gelejana kaShingana**; compare it with testimony before turning a proposed regency into an established roster. Their dates and roles remain provisional here. |
| **3** | Can Ntombela and other omitted northern houses improve the opening court? | Begin with **Lukwazi → Ndabankulu** and verify when Ndabankulu succeeded. Expand only when a constituency and a usable membership/succession link are both established. |

**Access qualification:** Kuper's and Ballard's full articles were not inspected; their accessible extracts and visible notes support only the narrow claims attributed to them. No uninspected archival file is represented as directly verified. Catalogue records were used for metadata, never as proof of a political claim. The bibliography records the editions actually consulted; older JSA ISBN-10s are retained when that is the identifier printed in the inspected volume, rather than guessing an ISBN-13.

## Recommendations

1. **Adopt the mixed historical catalogue**, with genealogical houses, royal establishments and personal followings explicitly distinguished. This preserves useful current cast work without making every ID hereditary.
2. **Anchor the opening court in the strongest names**, while visibly qualifying uncertain January 1836 heads. Do not open later nineteenth-century men at the height of their eventual power simply to fill rows.
3. **Separate the royal branches before adjusting head selection.** The important correction is the identity and accounting boundary; freezing a spokesperson cannot repair a merged dynasty.
4. **Keep a recognised head stable by default**, with explicit succession, regency, deposition, migration, exile, rival recognition and dissolution exceptions. Use court prominence to select a spokesperson only where appropriate to that entity.
5. **Preserve evidence labels at the relationship level:** documented/editorially established, attributed oral tradition, disputed, and authored alternate history. A well-supported actor can still have an uncertain father, accession date or successor.
6. **Implement the partition as a separate dated change of jurisdiction**, informed by existing local identities. Neither native generation nor a colonial appointment should create an “ancient great house” automatically.
