# Dunn and the Zulu succession cast, 1855–1879

**29 October 2026 — targeted historical research; no gameplay changes.**

## Executive summary

Use **1856 for Dunn's intervention for Mbuyazi** and **1857 for his settlement and developing relationship with Cetshwayo**. His earlier hunting and trading need a separate introduction. Ballard's scholarly account makes Dunn's subsequent wealth, clients and firearms access central to Cetshwayo's consolidation; it does not establish a regular annual delivery beginning in 1855. [1] Walmsley's contemporary letter confirms practical assistance to Dunn but disputes official government responsibility: a British military guarantee would overstate the evidence. [2]

**Hamu kaNzibe and Hamu kaMpande are one person.** Mayinga's testimony calls him kaMpande and associates his Mfemfe establishment with the continuation of Nzibe's house. [3, p. 252] Knight explains the distinction between biological parentage and genealogical house, and its restriction on Hamu's ordinary succession entitlement. [4, p. 3] A politically supported pretender is therefore defensible; automatic senior legitimate heir is not.

**Mthonga/uMtonga is kaMpande, not kaHamu.** The Stuart editors place his flight from Cetshwayo early in 1861. [3, p. 263, n. 89] Keep Zibhebhu's royal kinship separate from a demonstrated claim to the entire kingdom. His approximate 1841 birth and eventual Mandlakazi leadership do not support presenting him as an established adult rival king in 1855. [5, pp. 311–312]

## Introduction

The intended decision is how to pace an SB cast around the Cetshwayo–Mbuyazi struggle and its aftermath. “Second Zulu Civil War” is the conventional designation of the **1856** conflict; a further war in the 1860s would be an alternate-history development. The brief distinguishes historical appearance, later attested activity and proposed game availability. It does not prescribe new dates for existing character templates.

## Research approach

This was a targeted review on 29 October 2026, using the existing SB cast briefs as leads, exact-name web searches, Cambridge's article record, the University of Cape Town's eMandulo archival collections, institutional thesis repositories, SA History Online's hosted reference text and specialist historical-society publications. It was not a systematic literature review. Ballard's abstract and references were inspected, not his full article. Relevant passages of the other cited works were read. The archive's Ndabazezwe PDF repeatedly timed out; the older SB brief's specific editorial note was therefore not represented as newly rechecked evidence here.

## Findings

### Dunn: distinguish presence, intervention and service

| Stage | Evidence and implication |
|---|---|
| Earlier 1850s | Knight places Dunn's hunting residence after transport work in 1853, and his encounter with Walmsley in 1854. The account itself has compressed chronology, so **“already familiar with Zululand by the mid-1850s”** is safer than a precise first-arrival scene in 1855. [6, pp. 1–2] |
| Late 1856 | Dunn supported Mbuyazi and escaped the defeat. Walmsley's letter of **11 January 1857** says he lent Dunn muskets and personal guns, while denying that Dunn acted as a government servant. Stanley identifies this as a contested intervention involving traders' compensation claims. [2, §§1–3] |
| After the defeat | Dunn returned in connection with recovering traders' cattle; Cetshwayo subsequently employed him as secretary and adviser. This was accommodation after opposing him, rather than permanent exclusion or immediate execution. [2, §§2.13, 3] Ballard dates the settled relationship to **1857**. [1] |
| 1860s–1870s | Recurring procurement is historically plausible: Knight describes substantial trade, including Mozambique routes, and reluctant Natal approval of supply as a means of strengthening Cetshwayo. Those passages establish a continuing business, **not a guaranteed equal annual shipment or a documented 1855 contract**. [7, p. 2] |
| 1878–1879 | Dunn crossed to Natal at the end of 1878; Knight places his active recruitment for Chelmsford's relief expedition in March 1879. The post-war partition then enlarged and confirmed his territorial position. These are later choices, not his allegiance in 1856. [6, pp. 5–6] |

**Design interpretation:** an initial offer of contacts, armed followers or future purchasing is supportable. A repeatable annual purchase can be a transparent game abstraction, conditional on continued access and allegiance. Do not call it a historical fixed entitlement, a gift from Britain, or a supply figure established by this research. The 1870s import totals concern several routes and destinations; they cannot simply be divided by years to obtain Dunn's personal annual delivery. [7, p. 2]

### Rival identities and availability

“Earliest anchor” below means the earliest sufficiently specific activity established in this pass, not a claim to have found the first event in the person's life.

| Person | Age and identity evidence | Earliest useful anchor; constituency | Cast judgement |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hamu kaNzibe / kaMpande** | One prince, assigned to Nzibe's house. [3], [4] Laband gives **c.1834** and calls him Mpande's eldest son; the date/ranking combination needs further checking. [5, p. 109] | Supported Cetshwayo in 1856; Ngenetsheni leader. [5, p. 109] **June 1865:** Pretorius planned to offer him the throne; an assembled Boer force never implemented the scheme. [8, p. 40] | Introduce him as an adult royal notable before making him a rival. **1865 is a strong dated political hook**, not proof that Hamu accepted the offer. His later ambition cannot be backdated into lifelong opposition. |
| **Mthonga / Mtonga / uMtonga kaMpande** | Mpande's son and Cetshwayo's brother. No secure exact birth year established. [3, p. 263, n. 89] | **1861 flight**, followed by renewed escape in **1865** and further activity around 1872; his refuge made him important in Natal–Transvaal diplomacy. [8, pp. 41–43] | Best principal royal rival for an early-1860s crisis. Represent protection and expatriate connections; no separately quantified territorial following was established. |
| **Mkhungo / Mkungu kaMpande** | His own recorded testimony identifies Monase as his mother, Mbuyazi as the eldest son of their house and himself as its youngest child. Exact birth year unresolved. [9, p. 232] | The editors identify his placement with Colenso after 1856. [9, p. 233, n. 4] Later an exiled iziGqoza focus in Natal. [4, p. 2] | Strong continuity from Mbuyazi's defeated household. Begin with exile/protection; do not infer an independent adult army immediately after the battle. |
| **Zibhebhu kaMaphitha** | **c.1841**: about 14–15 in 1855–56. Supported Cetshwayo in 1856. [5, p. 311] His father Maphitha belonged to a collateral royal lineage descending from Jama. [10, p. 65] | Northern trading interests and tensions with his father; succeeds Maphitha in **1872** as eldest son of the Great House. Hayiyana was the eldest son overall, of another house. [10, p. 65] | Use a younger Mandlakazi figure first, then the chief. Do not count father and son as two full constituencies or turn collateral royal status into automatic crown eligibility. |
| **Sikhotha / Sikhota kaMpande** | A distinct prince in Knight's account; exact age unresolved. [4, pp. 1–3] | Mkhungo's émigré network; military representative in **1879**. [4, p. 3] | Supporting event actor first. His network overlaps Mkhungo's; no separate claimant bloc is needed. Do not silently merge him with Siteku/Sitheku. |
| **Dabulamanzi; Ndabuko; Ziwedu kaMpande** | Laband estimates births **c.1839, c.1843, c.1834**, respectively. Dabulamanzi and Ndabuko supported Cetshwayo in 1856; Ziwedu became a favoured half-brother. [5, pp. 59, 185, 312] | Later royal residences appear in Guy's political geography. [10, p. 65] | Useful relatives and later loyalist actors; royal birth alone does not make each an active pretender. Approximate teenage ages in 1856 must not receive their mature command careers. |

Ballard explicitly identifies renewed rivalries involving Cetshwayo, Hamu and “Zibepu” after 1856. [1] That is evidence against excluding Zibhebhu from politics altogether. It does **not**, on an abstract-only reading, establish a formal early bid for kingship. Guy distinguishes the Mandlakazi's collateral lineage and regional autonomy from Mpande's sons, and cautions that these tensions had not become a general internal war before the British invasion. [10, pp. 65–67]

### Traits and provenance

Use relationships and actions before personality labels. **Hamu's later Ambitious** is a reasonable interpretation of a contested royal career, not an attested psychological diagnosis. Dunn's mediation and procurement justify functional competence; they do not require an invented permanent loyalty trait. Zibhebhu's commercial independence can motivate decisions without making him “treacherous” from adolescence. Retain sparse trait packages for Mthonga and Mkhungo where the evidence describes position rather than temperament.

The Stuart material is **recorded oral history**, with named speakers and editorial intervention. Mkhungo's own testimony is particularly useful for household identity, while its recollections of earlier political events still require source criticism. Colonial correspondence is also interested evidence: Walmsley's denial of responsibility is not a neutral verdict. Neither source category should be converted into “legendary”, and no reconstructed scene should be presented as a recovered quotation.

## Discussion and recommendations

Recommended pacing is an authored synthesis of the anchors above:

1. **1855–1856:** keep the central contest concentrated on Cetshwayo and Mbuyazi. Dunn is a young intermediary who offers practical help. Hamu may support a brother; Maphitha represents Mandlakazi weight. Younger royals can appear as relatives without independent claim blocs.
2. **1857–1860:** make reconciliation with Dunn and the survival/exile of Monase's household the consequences. Let Dunn acquire clients and commercial importance gradually.
3. **1861:** make Mthonga the named focus of a royal-refuge and diplomatic crisis. Mkhungo supplies a separate Natal connection without needing a simultaneous second domestic rebellion.
4. **1865 onward:** use the proposed Hamu alternative as a conditional escalation. Boer backing is an offer with conditions and capacity limits. War or acceptance is counterfactual, not a historical certainty.
5. **1872–1873:** foreground Zibhebhu's actual succession and Cetshwayo's final consolidation. This gives chiefly autonomy, royal pretenders and access to firearms distinct stakes.
6. **1879 and the thirteen chiefs:** let wartime decisions rearrange the cast. Zibhebhu fought the British before submitting in August; Hamu defected, while Dunn's active British service followed his initial withdrawal. [5, pp. 109–110, 311]; [6, p. 5] Their later positions should not force the same three-man coalition decades earlier.

This sequence adds **Mthonga as the main new early-1860s rival**, uses **Mkhungo chiefly for continuity and exile**, and brings **Hamu forward at an evidenced political moment**. It leaves Zibhebhu's strongest independent role for his chiefly succession and the later settlement.

## Limitations and evidence gaps

- No secure exact birthday was established for Mthonga, Mkhungo or Sikhotha. Published approximate ages should not become precise biographies.
- No verified fixed annual gun quantity, precise 1855 appointment or formal British commitment to Mbuyazi was found.
- The 1865 Hamu scheme is documented through Cope's synthesis; its underlying McGill/colonial material was not independently examined. It proves a contemplated foreign policy, not Hamu's assent.
- Ballard's full article remains the priority follow-up for the precise chronology and financial detail of Dunn's rise. Its accessible abstract supports only the broad argument used here.
- A specialist society's separate unsigned/brief Dunn biography surfaced with obvious errors, including placing Dunn on Cetshwayo's side in 1856 and calling Zibhebhu his brother. It was excluded as evidence; sharing a host with better work does not establish accuracy.
